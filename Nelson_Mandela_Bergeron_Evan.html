Nelson Mandela is especially fascinating to me because he was on US Terrorist watchlists until 2008. The fact that the US government opposed his words and actions to such a degree is concerning, and can remind us to maintain a healthy level of skepticism regarding the way the governments label individuals and groups, especially individuals and groups which history is likely to absolve. Not only is it probably morally or ethically wrong to demonize legitimate individuals or groups who fight against oppressive systems, it also undermines the legitimacy and credibility of the government who hisory reveals to have been in opposition to legitimately uplifting, liberatory, or progressive political movements.

Nelson Mandela was president of the African National Congress from 1991 to 1997, and served as the first black president of South Africa from 1994 to 1999. He fought through a variety of means to end apartheid, the oppressive white supremecist system of South Africa that functioned somewhat similar to Jim Crow segregation, but a more accurate, present-day comparison might be with the conditions Palestinians live under, which, according to Human Rights Watch, B'Tselem, and Amnesty International, can be considered a system of apartheid. Nelson Mandela was imprisoned for 27 years for fighting apartheid, and eventually resumed political activies after his release. He advocated against oppression worldwide and, during a speech in 1997, even linked the struggle for freedom in South Africa with the struggle for freedom in Palestine.
